**Intended type:** Method

**Working title:** Which habitat maps can be trusted? Calibrated null tests
for thousands of DNA-defined North American fungi

Atlases now fit thousands of species distribution models automatically, but
report each map's skill with scores that clustered collecting inflates. The
usual safeguard, a null test against randomly placed occurrences, does not
work under realistic sampling. We measured this on virtual species collected
at the real survey sites of a continental community-science programme:
scattered nulls passed 73% of species whose records followed no habitat at
all, and every such species with 100 or more sites. Nulls that keep each
species' own clustering, restricted to surveyed sites, held false passes near
2% [E1: with intervals by sample size and habitat breadth], at a cost in power
that we quantify.

We use the calibrated test to grade every map of MycoMap Atlas, habitat
models for [N] fungal taxa defined by DNA barcodes rather than morphological
names, rebuilt nightly from validated community-science collections. In a
hindcast, maps graded strong placed 91% of records validated after the
training cutoff in their top half, against 78% for failed maps (95%
interval of the difference 9–17 points), and beat a map of distance to
known records by 4 points (1–8), an advantage that came from finds more
than 100 km away; weak maps did no better than distance. A bar against
chance, which even failed maps clear, cannot judge such maps; distance to
known records can. The paper gives best-practice guidance for null
tests in target-group designs, compares the approach with default practice
and with recent structure-keeping nulls, and documents the atlas as an open,
versioned resource (code, layers, models and training-record identifiers on
Zenodo).
